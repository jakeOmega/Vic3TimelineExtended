# AI Weights and Company AI

Part of the [mod systems reference](../mod_systems.md), which indexes every system.

## AI Weights Guidelines

- **Journal entries:** `weight` determines priority in the JE list. Global issues use 1000+, personal/national issues use 10.
- **Scripted buttons:** `ai_chance = { value = 0 ... }` — base value with conditional `add` modifiers.
- **Diplomatic actions:** Key fields: `evaluation_chance`, `will_propose`, `propose_score`, `will_break`.
- **Treaty articles:** Key fields: `article_ai_usage = { offer request none }`, `evaluation_chance`, `inherent_accept_score`.
- Always look at **vanilla files** for AI pattern reference.

## Company AI

The engine picks one company at a time for the AI to work toward: among the types whose `ai_will_do` holds (and at least `attainable`), weighted by `ai_weight` (engine default 1) times its `NAI` `COMPANY_TYPE_*` factors (stickiness ×3 for 60 months, a formable bonus, predicted productivity, competition with companies already held). While planning it builds toward the type's `ai_construction_targets`. Numbers live in `common/script_values/te_company_ai_values.txt`.

- **Weights.** Vanilla gives its flavored companies 3 and generic ones nothing (1). The mod's 84 flavored companies get `te_company_ai_weight_flavored` (10), plus `te_company_ai_weight_home` (10) while the country holds one of the company's `preferred_headquarters` states; its 11 new generic companies get `te_company_ai_weight_generic` (3). `REPLACE:`/`INJECT:` entries are vanilla's companies and keep vanilla's weight.
- **Construction targets need a `state_trigger`.** Vanilla's `companies.md`: a target with an empty state trigger does nothing. Every mod target now names the company's preferred HQ states, the capital, or a state that already has the building (that last one covers mines and rigs whose resource the capital lacks). `test_company_ai.py` fails on a target without one, a mod flavored company without `ai_weight`, or a buildable flagship without `ai_value`.
- **Flagships** (`common/buildings/company_buildings.txt`) carry `ai_value = te_company_flagship_ai_value` (50000, vanilla's top tier). Their only max level comes from the company's prosperity bonus (on at 100, off below 75), so only a prosperous company's flagship can be built at all. Only the state's owner builds one (`te_company_flagship_builder_owns_state` in `possible` and `can_build_government`), so investment rights abroad never reach another country's flagship. Before the `ai_value`, the AI built 9% (2029 observer save) to 14% (2073 save) of its prosperous companies' flagships. If `ai_value` doesn't move that, the fallback is a decision that places the flagship (the Strategic Reserve hub and Space Program precedent).
- **Failing companies.** The engine's AI never disbands a company by choice (observer run 1972–2029: no surviving country dropped one company for another), so its slots fill before the mod's late-era companies unlock. `te_company_failing_monthly_effect` (`te_company_effects.txt`, on `on_monthly_pulse_country`) counts each company's consecutive monthly checks that fail either test (`te_company_fails_this_month`): prosperity below `te_company_failing_prosperity` (10), or owned levels (`company_owned_levels`) below `te_company_small_share` (0.1%) of the country's `building_levels`, saved as `scope:te_company_min_levels` before the iteration. The count lives in the company variable `te_company_months_failing`; an AI country removes one company a check once a count reaches `te_company_failing_months` (60, about 4.9 years), then runs the flagship cleanup. The size test catches a prosperous company too small to matter to a large economy; since the engine dissolves companies under 5 levels anyway, it only bites above 5,000 country levels. Together they stand in for "profit below 0.1% of GDP for five years", which script cannot read (no company profit trigger or link): on the 2073 save, of 406 AI companies with five years of history, 102 fell under the profit rule; prosperity alone flags 50 (41 of the 102), both tests 83 (52 of the 102, the rest earning a median 0.34% of GDP). Players are exempt; it models the AI choosing what a player chooses in the companies panel.
- **Flagship subsidies.** The engine's AI almost never changes a subsidy itself. It keeps the ones a building gets by default (ports, railways, power plants) and adds none: on the 2073 save it subsidized 0 of its 23 company buildings (the player 20 of 20), 0 of 140 shipyards, and 0 of 250 airports and highways outside the laws that force them. 12 of the 23 were under half staffed, some losing workers to subsidized buildings nearby, since a building that is not subsidized stops hiring below `BUILDING_DEFAULT_MIN_EARNINGS_TO_HIRE_EMPLOYEES`. `te_company_ai_subsidy_monthly_effect` (`te_company_effects.txt`, same monthly on-action) subsidizes every `bg_company_buildings` building of an AI country that is not in default, and removes those subsidies in default, because the AI never drops one on its own (in default it still paid for 60 of 61 railways). A subsidy only tops a building up to `SUBSIDIES_MIN_GAIN` (0), so a profitable flagship costs nothing. Players are exempt, as for dissolution. The mod's `country_building_*_require_subsidies_bool` forced-subsidy modifiers (airports and highways under Interventionism and Command Economy) held on only 35 of 52 such AI buildings in that save, so this uses `set_subsidized` on each building instead.
- **Verifying in an observer run:** company counts per country and companies lost while the country survived, from saves (the 2026-10-06 study's decoders: company records key `company_type` 0x5c80, prosperity 14193, `prosperous` 24210), and flagship counts with `scripts/analysis/save_world_report.py buildings`. Flagship subsidies: in a plain-text save each building record under `building_manager` carries `subsidized=yes` when subsidized; an AI flagship should have it unless its country is `in_default`.

## Law-Change Cadence

AI countries start law changes about as often as a player would: an AI with a law it wants seldom waits more than a few months after finishing one enactment before starting the next (2026-11-15). Before, a conservative AI waited about four years, which left most governments enacting little of what their movements asked for; a Workshop player reported that the reformist side could hardly win vanilla's Reform or Revolution entry. The owner ruled that frequent AI law changes are fine ("even if the AI is enacting laws 100% of the time it is probably fine", 2026-11-15), so the rate is sized for rarely waiting. The aversion to civil-war laws rises with it, so that more law changes don't bring more civil wars.

**Files.** `common/ai_strategies/edited_default_strategy.txt`: `ai_strategy_default`'s `change_law_chance` is vanilla's 1 plus 39, times 10 in the mod's crisis case (a movement with over 20% support at over 0.75 radicalism). `common/ai_strategies/edited_political_strategies.txt`: `revolution_aversion` in seven political agendas. `test_ai_law_cadence.py` pins both and holds their restated vanilla lines to the game's files. In-game checks: `docs/testing/ai-economy-playtest.md` § AI law cadence.

**How the engine runs it.**
- **One roll every 50 days.** Each country has a law update every 1,200 hours (50 days) at a phase of its own. At it the AI starts changing a law with chance `change_law_chance` (1 = 1%). This is measured, not documented: across the saves of one game, every pair of one country's enactment starts is a whole number of 50-day periods apart (99% of the pairs among 155 starts of 46 countries in eleven saves, 1895–1958; 94% among 76 starts of 24 countries in six saves of a second game), and the greatest common divisor of one country's start-to-start gaps is 1,200 hours for most of them. A 100-day period fits only 44–46% of the pairs. `scripting_best_practices.md` § The AI Rolls `change_law_chance` Every 50 Days.
- **The chance is the default strategy's plus the political strategy's, by vanilla's comment (unread in game).** Vanilla's comment on the default's field: "Using this value in other strategies will function additively". If it is wrong and an agenda's own field replaces the default's, the change reaches no agenda; that is cause (b) of the self-check's BROKEN. Vanilla 1.15.1's agendas add 1 (Mandate of Heaven), 2.5 (conservative; +7.5 with an insurrectionary interest group), 3.5 (reactionary; +1.5), 10 (progressive, nationalist, consolidation; +10, +10, +5), 15 (egalitarian; +5), 20 (confrontation; +5), 50 (Meiji Restoration) and 100 (Tanzimat, Great Reforms), plus 25 under revolutionary socialist laws and 10–25 under a law commitment. So, if the comment holds, raising the default's field raises every agenda by the same amount.
- **Which law.** A successful roll weighs the laws the country could start: +100 for each ideology of an interest group the strategy favours that wants it, −25 for each of one it opposes, 1,000 × support × radicalism for each movement that wants it, plus the law's `ai_enact_weight_modifier`, with randomness (`NAI|CHANGE_LAW_*`). `min_law_chance_to_pass` drops a law whose base chance to pass is below it (the agenda's value replaces the default's 50: 10 to 20, often halved when the AI has reasons not to oppose the law), and `max_progressiveness` and `max_regressiveness` cap how far it moves. A country enacts one law at a time.
- **Civil-war laws.** A movement that would defend the current law at 0.70 radicalism or more makes the change a possible civil war (`CHANGE_LAW_POTENTIAL_CIVIL_WAR_THRESHOLD`). The defines name two gates: at the start the AI goes ahead with chance 100 − `revolution_aversion` and remembers a refusal for 24 months; during the enactment it cancels once its aversion, times 1 + 0.005 × the revolutionary movement's support (× 5 for a subject), beats a boldness drawn from 0–100 for that law and kept 24 months. `revolution_aversion` is per agenda (its value replaces the default's 5). It has a third use: `CHANGE_LAW_CIVIL_WAR_AVERSION_LAW_DIRECTION_FLEXIBILITY` (2) adds twice the aversion to the `max_progressiveness` or `max_regressiveness` the AI accepts "to avoid a revolution", so a conservative AI facing one takes a law up to 200 progressiveness away under vanilla's 75. Vanilla's comment on the default's field gives a fourth reading, "Chance each day that the AI will stop enacting a law that is going to spark a civil war (1 aversion should translate into ~25% chance of avoiding a civil war)"; the defines' two gates are the more specific account, and the comment's daily chance is not used here.

**The table.** Expected wait from the end of one enactment to the start of the next, for an AI with a law it can start: half a period to the next roll, then 50 days per failed roll, so 25 + 50 × (1 − p) / p days. Start to start adds the enactment itself, about 15 months (below). "Mod before" is the mod before 2026-11-15, whose default field was vanilla's 1 in normal times and 10 in its crisis case.

The new rate stands on the normal-times column alone. The crisis branch only makes a start certain. It will fire less often if #1824 merges, which puts `MOVEMENT_RADICALISM_FROM_ACTIVE_LAW_STANCE` back from the mod's −0.1 to vanilla's −0.05: under −0.1, a 1907 observer save had 17 of 70 socialist and labor movements at 0.75 radicalism or more. The saves below were read under −0.1. Vanilla's own insurrection case (an insurrectionary interest group) takes a conservative AI to 11% (14 months) and a progressive or nationalist one to 21% (7 months); under the new rate it adds the same points to a chance already near half.

**What the saves show for the mod before.** In eleven saves of four games (1864–2020, `scripts/analysis/ai_law_cadence_report.py`), 22–31% of the AI countries that change laws at all were in the middle of an enactment; decentralized nations never do (121 of the 123 AI countries in an 1864 save that had changed none). By agenda, pooled: conservative 19% (of 1,247), reactionary 21% (347), egalitarian 38% (226), progressive 42% (106), nationalist 43% (450). Conservatives are most AI countries (163 of 210 in 1864, 96 of 250 in 1958), so they set the world's pace. Enactments that passed between two saves took a median of 17 months (13 months to 5 years); that sample favours long ones, so the table uses 15. With 15 months, the wait formula predicts 24% for conservatives and 51% for progressives and nationalists if every AI always had a law to start, against 19% and 42–43% read: the model fits, and roughly a fifth of the time an AI has nothing it would start. Counted over every AI country that is not decentralized, as the self-check counts (countries that never changed a law stay in; `ai_law_cadence_report.py`'s "self-check count" line), 20 saves of six games read conservatives at 0.00–0.26 and the rest at 0.21–0.47; early saves read lowest (all AI countries 0.11 in 1849 and 0.12 in 1867, when few have a law to start), and from 1890 conservatives read 0.06–0.26 and the rest 0.26–0.43.

**The rate: 40 in the default strategy.** A roll at 41–60% puts the wait at two to three months, a sixth of an enactment's length, so an AI with something to enact is enacting about five sixths of the time. Doubling it to certain would shorten the wait to under a month and add about 15% more starts, at the cost of the agendas' spread and of the crisis case meaning anything. The default's field is the route taken, though it carries one unread fact the agenda route doesn't, that the default's field adds to an agenda's own. An INJECT into each of eleven agendas would rest on the other unread fact, that an INJECT reaches a strategy's field, eleven times over, restate eleven vanilla fields, and collide with another mod's INJECTs of the same fields. It also lifts Mandate of Heaven, which vanilla holds at 1 on purpose, to 41% (fine, by the owner's ruling as the wave's coordinator relayed it, 2026-11-15); that strategy still starts only laws with at least a 50% chance to pass (10% when it has reasons not to oppose one), moves at most 25 progressiveness and 2 regressiveness, and its aversion stays 100. The field restates vanilla's `value = 1` and adds 39 on its own line, so it reads 40 whether the engine replaces vanilla's field with the INJECTed one (as an interest group's field does) or merges in only the keys vanilla's lacks (as a strategy's `subsidies` block does).

**The pass-chance floor: kept.** Progressive and nationalist agendas roll at the same 11% and differ in floor, 10 against 20, and the saves read 42% and 43% of each mid-enactment: no sign the floor binds. With the rate no longer the bottleneck, a lower floor would add attempts at laws below a 20% chance, most of which fail and lock that law for two years (`LAW_ENACTMENT_COOLDOWN_DAYS`). If conservatives stay well under their predicted share after the change (below), the floor or `max_progressiveness` is what binds, and the play-test list says so.

**Revolution aversion: raised in seven agendas.** A faster AI meets each civil-war law more often within the 24 months its boldness and its refusal last, so its yearly exposure rises by the rise in enactment starts per 24 months. That rise is not the chance's ratio, because one law at a time caps the starts: a conservative AI goes from 0.39 starts per 24 months to 1.35. The factor lies between the start ratio (when the risky law is a rare pick) and the ratio of the chances of at least one start in 24 months (when it is the first pick). With both gates counted, the chance to go ahead and not cancel is (1 − aversion / 100)², so the new aversion is 100 − (100 − vanilla) / √factor, at the band's geometric middle (the "middle" column, unrounded), rounded to 5. With one gate the chance is 1 − aversion / 100 and the aversion that holds exposure at vanilla's is 100 − (100 − vanilla) / factor, the last column:

Mandate of Heaven (aversion 100: it never goes ahead), the Meiji Restoration (its starts rise by 6%), the Tanzimat and the Great Reforms (already certain) keep vanilla's. Each INJECT restates vanilla's whole block, conditionals and `min` included, and adds the rise as one `add` line, for the same two readings as the rate. If the engine instead summed the two fields, aversion would come out 160 for conservatives, 155 for consolidation, 120 for reactionaries and 110 for progressives: those AIs would never pass a law that a radical movement (0.70 or more) defends, which blocks contested reforms outright, against what the change is for, and the self-check would still read OK. It is unlikely (an interest group's field is replaced, not summed); the play-test's civil-war row watches for it as "far fewer, near none". The flexibility define above widens the concessions an AI accepts to avoid a revolution by twice each rise, 10 to 40 points (a reactionary facing one goes from 125 to 165 progressiveness, a conservative from 200 to 220, against laws that span −100 to 200): small, and in the direction the change wants, so it changes no value here.

**Systems that wait for a gap between enactments.** Four of the mod's systems act only while a country is not enacting a law: the cultural-hegemony law-pressure event (`cultural_hegemony.16`), the judiciary's events 35 and 36, and its monthly court challenge (`te_jud_challenge_monthly`, which takes a forced law to the court once the enactment is over). An AI enacting most of the time gives them fewer windows; a player's are unchanged. One edge, read from the script and not run: `te_jud_open_challenge` keeps one pending case in `te_jud_challenge_law`, so if a second forced law starts before an idle month comes, it overwrites the first, and the first escapes review. That needs two forced laws inside the capacity gate, so it is rare and AI-only; nothing is changed for it.

**What is unread.**
- Whether an INJECT reaches an AI strategy's script-value field at all. Before this change the default's INJECT read 1 in normal times either way, and the one read on a strategy, `subsidies`, kept vanilla's value (`scripting_best_practices.md`). No trigger or script value reads a strategy's value (`has_strategy` says only which strategy a country holds), so the self-check `TE_SELF_CHECK ai_law_cadence` reads the effect: in 1890 or later, the share of AI conservatives mid-enactment, and of the rest. Only its OK settles anything. The build before read conservatives at 0.06–0.26 from 1890 (20 saves of six games, counted as the check counts), and OK is 0.40. But a working change can read as low as 0.23 if, after each enactment, an AI goes a long stretch with no law it would start, so a BROKEN has three causes: the INJECT is ignored, the default's rate doesn't add to an agenda's own (every agenda declares one), or nothing to start. The play-test row says how to tell them apart.
- The 50-day period is read from saves, not from a define or the documentation.
- Whether the two civil-war gates compound or share one boldness draw. If they share one, exposure is the "one gate" column: up to two thirds above vanilla for conservatives.
- The enactment length, which moves the factors little: at 10 or 20 months the conservative factor's middle is 3.2 or 2.5.

**With the third-party AI mod that a Workshop player runs alongside** (its files: `kai_political_strategies.txt`, a whole-file copy of `00_default_strategy.txt`; from the 2026-11-15 study of it, not from its files). It INJECTs `change_law_chance`, `min_law_chance_to_pass` and `revolution_aversion` into the five common agendas, the Tanzimat and the Great Reforms, and REPLACEs the Meiji Restoration. Its copy of the default strategy keeps vanilla's `change_law_chance = { value = 1 }`, and this mod's INJECT lands on that copy, so the rates add: each agenda's chance is that mod's agenda value plus this mod's 40. On `revolution_aversion` both mods INJECT the same field. INJECTs apply in file order and, by the interest-group read, the later one wins; `edited_political_strategies.txt` sorts before `kai_political_strategies.txt`, so that mod's values stand and this mod's land only on the consolidation and confrontation agendas it doesn't touch. Neither mod's aversions were sized for the combined rate: that mod's were set for its own agenda rates, which now carry this mod's 39 on top. The extra exposure is probably small, since that mod's waits are already short. Its floors stand, since this mod sets none. An INJECT can't survive a REPLACE, but this mod injects nothing into the Meiji Restoration. That two INJECTs of one strategy field resolve this way is unread; if the earlier one wins instead, this mod's aversions apply with both mods' rates added, a higher rate than these aversions were sized for.

## AI Coverage of the Mod's Content

The engine's AI learns about content from fields on that content: a goods stance in a strategy, an `ai_value` on a building, an `ai_enact_weight_modifier` on a law, a movement list in a political strategy, an `ai_weight` on a mobilization option. Content with none of them is not broken; the AI treats it as it treats a plain factory or a law nobody asked for. #1822 listed where the mod's own content had none while vanilla's equivalent has one (2026-11-15). Each part below says what was added, what vanilla value it stands on, and what was left alone and why. Sizes that no vanilla value forces are named script values in `common/script_values/te_ai_coverage_values.txt`, for tuning after an observer run; the in-game checks are in `docs/testing/ai-economy-playtest.md` § AI coverage of the mod's content.

**Goods stances** (`common/ai_strategies/edited_default_strategy.txt`, `te_ai_export_stance_strategies.txt`; `test_ai_goods_stances.py`). A stance tells the AI it wants a good in high supply, wants to export it or does not want it. A wanted or exported good is "favored": the AI adds to the score of a building or a production method that makes it (`NAI` `PRODUCTION_BUILDING_FAVORED_GOODS_FACTOR` 0.5 and `PRODUCTION_METHOD_FAVORED_GOODS_FACTOR` 0.25 per pound of it), and a high-supply good also counts for more when its price is high and when the AI weighs a subsidy. Five of the mod's goods had no stance, and one input of the later construction tiers had none:

| Good | Stance | From (technology) | Why that gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| `tech_metals` | high supply, default strategy | `personal_appliances` | the Consumer Appliances Industry's base method is their first user |
| `consumer_appliances` | high supply, default strategy; export, industrial strategy (from `personal_appliances`) | `integrated_circuits` | Integrated Circuits is their first heavy industrial user (20 a level; 37 methods use them in all); before, they are a household good |
| `motor_ships` | high supply, default strategy, countries with a port at major power or above | `advanced_submarine_technology`, until `modern_material_science` | vanilla's `steamers` stance one generation on, retired as vanilla retires clippers |
| `magnetic_drive_ships` | the same | `modern_material_science` | the next generation |
| `automobiles` | high supply, default strategy | `prefabricated_construction` | Advanced Construction uses 10 a level |
| `tourism` | export, agricultural and plantation strategies | `television_broadcasting` (Mass Tourism) | vanilla's luxuries (wine, liquor, coffee, tea) are exported by the same two strategies; no building uses tourism |

- **`grid_power` has no stance on purpose.** It is a fixed-price settlement good that can't be traded between markets, so nothing a stance does could move it. The test's `EXEMPT` list holds it with that reason.
- **The other inputs of the later construction tiers already had stances.** Reinforced Concrete, Advanced Construction, Nanomaterial and Self-Replicating Construction add engines, automobiles, digital assets, advanced materials and robotics to vanilla's tiers and use more copper and electricity. Vanilla's default strategy wants copper and electricity from `electrical_generation` and engines from `railways`; the mod's wanted digital assets, robotics and advanced materials already, each gated on a technology the tier's own technology needs first (the test walks the prerequisites). Steel, glass, explosives, tools and transportation have no default stance in vanilla either, though vanilla's own construction tiers use them.
- **Vanilla's `steamers` stance can't be retired.** Motor ships replace steamers in ports and fishing fleets, and vanilla's stance (major powers with a port, from `ironclad_tech` and `gantry_cranes`) never ends. An INJECT into the default strategy can't change a key vanilla's block already holds (read for `subsidies`, 2026-10-16), and the executable holds four stance values (`wants_high_supply`, `wants_export`, `wants_import`, `does_not_want`), none of which means "no stance", so a strategy-level override could only turn steamers into a good the AI avoids. What the new stances do is end the tilt: before, a shipyard's steamer methods scored as favored and its motor-ship methods did not.
- **Unread in game:** that an INJECT adds a new key to a strategy's `goods_stances` block. It does for `subsidies`, the same shape, and the default strategy's seven older mod stances rest on the same fact. No trigger reads a stance, so there is no self-check; the play-test rows read the effect.
- **With the third-party AI mod** that REPLACEs six of the seven vanilla administrative strategies, the three export stances are lost (a REPLACE outlasts every INJECT). The default strategy's stances stay: that mod's default strategy is a bare copy, which INJECTs land on.

**Building `ai_value`** (`common/script_values/te_ai_coverage_values.txt`, `common/scripted_triggers/te_ai_coverage_triggers.txt`; `test_ai_building_values.py`). A building with no `ai_value` scores the engine's base, 1000, so a missing one is parity with a plain factory, not a fault. What was missing is the value vanilla gives the same role. A 2058 save (the owner's game, almost all AI) shows the scale of the need: 495 Renewable Energy Plants, 451 Fusion Plants, 149 Hydro Plants and 129 Nuclear Plants beside 423 coal and oil plants; 528 Highways, 464 Airports and 522 Network Infrastructure beside 580 Railways; but 16 of the 37 wonders, and megaproject sites for two of the seven megaprojects. So the power and infrastructure values are parity, and the wonder value is the one most likely to show.

| Buildings | `ai_value` | Vanilla's value for the role |
|---|---|---|
| Hydro, Nuclear, Renewable Energy and Fusion Plants | `te_clean_power_plant_ai_value`: 1000, plus 4000 in a state with no power plant of any kind built or under way | `building_power_plant`: 1000, plus 4000 in a state with no `building_power_plant` |
| vanilla's Power Plant (INJECT, the whole field restated) | the same test, `te_state_has_a_power_plant`, in place of vanilla's own | so a state with a nuclear plant draws no coal plant for the bonus, and the reverse |
| Highway, Airport, Network Infrastructure | `te_infrastructure_ai_value`, 2000 | `building_railway`'s base, 2000 |
| the 37 wonders, the UN Headquarters, a Power Bloc Headquarters | `te_wonder_ai_value`, 10000 | every monument a country can build (Eiffel Tower, Big Ben, …): 10000 |
| the seven megaprojects' construction sites, the Antimatter Warhead Plant | `te_megaproject_ai_value`: 1000, and 0 for a country in default or under a declared bankruptcy | the engine's base for a government building, which they scored before |

- **`te_state_has_a_power_plant`** counts vanilla's plant, the mod's four and the Solar Receiver, built or under construction. Electricity is a local good, so a state with no plant can't run a method that uses it and reads as needing none; that is what vanilla's 4000 is for. The test fails if a building joins the power group without joining the trigger.
- **Skipped, with the reason.**
  - *The four megaprojects #1822 named* (Antimatter Containment Facility, Consciousness Network, Orbital Battlestation, Orbital Solar Collector) are never started from the queue: `potential` asks for the building itself and its only max level is the one its own method grants per level, so script places and raises them when a construction site completes (`generic_wonder_construction_base`). What the queue starts is the site, and the sites had no `ai_value` either. The Space Program is placed by its decision. The test's `PLACED_BY_SCRIPT` holds all five.
  - *The 13 discoverable mines.* Vanilla's iron, coal, lead and sulfur mines carry `ai_value = 1000`, which is the engine's base, and its copper mine none; only the gold mine has more (5000). The mod's mines are at parity already. What they lacked was a stance for what they dig: `tech_metals` has one now.
  - *The megaprojects' value is not raised.* A site's inputs run to hundreds of millions a week, paid by the government, and most AI countries in the October observer run were bankrupt by 2029. The value only stops a country in default from starting one.
- **Unread in game:** that an INJECTed `ai_value` replaces a vanilla building's (a script-value field does on an interest group and a party; a plain field does on a building). If the engine keeps vanilla's field, the coal plant still earns the bonus in a state with only a clean plant, as before. If it summed the two fields, the coal plant's base and bonus would double; the play-test row watches for that.

**Institution laws and the AI's bureaucracy** (`te_ai_coverage_triggers.txt`, `te_ai_coverage_values.txt`, `common/laws/extra_laws.txt`; `test_ai_institution_laws.py`). 26 of the mod's laws carry an institution: 16 ministry laws, National Bank, Central Bank Independence, Neocolonialism, Private Policing, Civic Monolingualism, Universal Basic Income, Post-Scarcity and three augmentation laws. Each level of an institution costs bureaucracy for as long as it exists (1 for each 100,000 people in incorporated states, at least 10), and a country short of bureaucracy loses tax revenue. None of the 26 weights looked at bureaucracy; the National Bank's standing +600 applied to a country already short.

- **One shared trigger.** `te_ai_can_staff_new_institution` holds while the country has the spare bureaucracy that level 1 of a new institution takes (`te_ai_new_institution_bureaucracy`: total population ÷ 100,000, at least 10) and its existing institutions are not heading for a shortage (`approaching_bureaucracy_shortage`, the engine's forecast). Vanilla's own test for a bureaucracy in order is the pair `bureaucracy >= 0`, `approaching_bureaucracy_shortage = no`; this adds the new institution's cost to the first.
- **One branch per law.** Each of the 26 `ai_enact_weight_modifier` blocks adds `te_ai_unstaffed_institution_law_weight` (−1000) while `te_ai_law_brings_unstaffed_institution = { INSTITUTION = … }` holds: the country does not yet have that institution and can't staff a new one. A country that already has the institution is untouched, so moving from Old Age Pension to Universal Basic Income, or from one Ministry of Labor to the other, weighs as before: the institution's bureaucracy is a running cost it pays already.
- **The size.** Vanilla's readme calls +1000 "very significant" beside a base weight in the low hundreds. −1000 outweighs a ministry's standing +10 to +200, the National Bank's +600 and a movement's push; the bank's rank and debt terms (+300, +400) or a +750 push still bring it out ahead, so it is a strong preference against, not a ban.
- **It does not slow law changes.** The AI picks among the laws it could start; a law weighed down leaves the others. The owner's ruling that frequent AI law changes are fine stands (§ Law-Change Cadence).
- **Left out:** vanilla's Migration Controls, which the mod gives an institution by INJECT. That institution costs a tenth of a normal one, and the check would mean restating a vanilla law's weight by INJECT (unread, and another AI mod INJECTs vanilla laws' weights too).

**The AI's institution-spending defines: left at vanilla's, with the arithmetic.** Vanilla's `NAI` block caps what the AI puts into institutions: `MAX_INSTITUTION_SPENDING_BASE` 0.015 ("AI will not spend more BUR than this on institutions (scaled by weekly GDP)") plus `MAX_INSTITUTION_SPENDING_PER_INSTITUTION` 0.005 "for each institution a country has". The AI raises an institution while spending stays at or under the ceiling (`INSTITUTION_SPENDING_INCREASE_SPENDING_RATIO` 1.0) and lowers one only at twice the ceiling (`…_DECREASE_SPENDING_RATIO` 2.0). The mod has 24 institutions to vanilla's 7 and raised `MAX_INSTITUTION_INVESTMENT` from 5 to 9, and set none of these.

| `…_PER_INSTITUTION` | Ceiling at 7 institutions | at 12 | at 17 | at 24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.005 (vanilla, in force) | 5.0% | 7.5% | 10.0% | 13.5% |
| 0.003 | 3.6% | 5.1% | 6.6% | 8.7% |
| 0.002 | 2.9% | 3.9% | 4.9% | 6.3% |
| 0.0015 | 2.6% | 3.3% | 4.1% | 5.1% |

The figures are shares of weekly GDP; the AI starts cutting at twice each (10% at vanilla's 7, 27% at 24). So the ceiling already grows with the number of institutions: it was not sized for seven in a way that breaks at 24. What changes is the total: a country holding all 24 may put 2.7 times as much into institutions as a vanilla country holding all 7, and cuts only at 27% of GDP. Holding a 24-institution country to vanilla's 5.0% would take 0.00146 per institution.

- **What the ceiling is a share of is not in any file.** Read as bureaucracy points per pound of weekly GDP it never binds, in vanilla or here (a country of 14 million people with £12M of GDP a year, roughly Prussia at the start, would be allowed about 11,500 points against about 4,900 for every vanilla institution at its top level), so the define would do nothing. Read as money (the cost of the bureaucracy the institutions use, as a share of weekly GDP) it binds where vanilla's AI is seen to hold back: the institution levels a country can afford come to 1,923 × ceiling × GDP per head ÷ pounds per bureaucracy point, about 6 of 35 levels for that country at £15 a point (the example's figures are round numbers, not read from a save). The money reading is the one that fits, and is inferred.
- **In money, on that reading:** an AI country with all 24 institutions may spend up to 13.5% of its GDP on the bureaucracy behind them, out of a government income that is itself only a part of GDP (not measured here). With weekly GDP of £50M that is £6.75M a week against £2.5M under vanilla's seven, and no cut before £13.5M.
- **In bureaucracy:** for a poor late-game country (£5 of GDP a head) the ceiling buys about 32 institution levels at £40 a point and 87 at £15, of up to 216 (24 × 9); at vanilla's seven institutions it would buy 12 and 32 of 35. For a rich one (£50 a head) it buys more levels than exist in both cases, and only produced bureaucracy limits it. A point's cost is the unknown: a Government Administration level on Internet Communications makes 220 points for £2,640 of goods a week (£12 a point) plus the wages of 500 to 750 staff, which no file gives.
- **Why nothing was changed.** The case rests on the money reading and on what AI countries actually spend, and neither has been measured. The October observer run had `declared_bankruptcy` on 126 of 179 AI countries by 2029 with institution levels falling alongside, and nobody has shown the cause. A lower ceiling also cuts the levels of the institutions the mod wants the AI to fund (the National Bank and the Ministry of the Environment score 100). The next observer run should read, for each AI country, its institutions and their levels, bureaucracy produced and used, and its Government Administrations' cost against GDP (the play-test row). If countries sit near the ceiling and those near it are the bankrupt ones, 0.003 is the first step (8.7% at 24 institutions, vanilla's figure for about fourteen).
